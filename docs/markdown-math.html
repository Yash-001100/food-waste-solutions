Markdown Math

The idea: for every product nearing expiry, calculate the minimum discount, applied at the optimal moment, that clears it before it becomes waste — learned per store, not applied as one blanket rule.

Everything below is built on real Walmart sales & price history (M5, Kaggle), filtered to groceries. Shelf life, product names and barcodes are disclosed synthetic layers — no public dataset tracks real expiry at SKU level.

01

Does a discount actually move it?

A fixed-effects regression on each store's real price history — controlling for the item's own baseline, day-of-week, month, SNAP and events — isolates how much demand responds when price moves, independent of everything else going on. This is the number the whole optimizer depends on.

2 of 10 stores didn't behave. TX_2 showed no statistically detectable price effect; CA_3 came back positive (quantity rising with price — almost certainly reverse causality: the store raises prices in response to strong demand). Both were flagged and replaced with the pooled median (−0.94) rather than trusted as-is.
02

The real discount-response curve

Every store-item-day, bucketed by how deep the real discount was, showing average sales relative to that item's own normal volume (dashed line = 1.0×, i.e. no lift). Built from real M5 price variation — not simulated.

Limitation worth remembering: over 95% of store-item-days in this data sit at full price — real discounts deeper than ~15% are rare. That's enough to fit a real curve, but using it to predict behavior at 30–50% clearance-style markdowns means extrapolating beyond what's directly observed — a standard constant-elasticity assumption, not a proven fact from this dataset.

04

Forecast accuracy

LightGBM, trained on ~5M rows across 10 stores, validated on the most recent 28 days — against a naive "same as last week" baseline.

WMAPE looks high (61%) in isolation — that's expected, not a red flag: 47% of store-item-days have zero units sold, so a miss of 1 unit on a 1-unit day is a "100% error" despite being trivial in absolute terms. This is exactly why the real M5 competition uses a custom weighted metric (WRMSSE) instead of raw percentage error.
